# The interstellar medium

In all galaxies, the space between celestial bodies is filled with a collection of gas and dust particles. These particles are known as *interstellar medium* (ISM) and possesses density of $10^{-9}$ grains$\times$cm$^{-3}$.{{< cite "wakelamrev">}} The ISM is a reservoir of constituents for the formation of new stars. It is mostly composed of hydrogen atoms, the second most common element is helium with a relative abundance of 8.5$\times 10^{-2}$ per hydrogen atom, followed by oxygen (2.6$\times 10^{-4}$per hydrogen atom{{< cite "Markwick_2000">}}) and carbon (1.2$\times 10^{-4}$ {{< cite "cardelli_1993">}} per hydrogen atom). All other elements are present with abundances lower than $10^{-4}$.  

The ISM floats in a sea of electromagnetic and particle radiations. Massive stars are the main sources of ultraviolet and visible radiation, and after absorption and re-emission by dust, they create a strong infrared field. Novae and supernovae are sources of high energy X-ray and $\gamma$-ray  radiation. Cosmic rays are fast-moving atomic nuclei (mostly protons) with average energies of the order of a few MeV. They are the only particles able to penetrate into  the denser dusty regions and ionize the $\ce{H2}$ molecule. The subsequent ion-electron recombination is then responsible for the weak ultraviolet radiation field inside the dust itself.  

Most of the interstellar volume is occupied by  \ce{H^+} ions. This gas surrounds cooler regions where chemical processes are possible. The *diffuse clouds* are the first of those regions: the presence of **dust grain** carried with gas provides a defence against the interstellar radiation field. More complex processes are observed in the *translucent clouds*, where the large density (1000 particle$\times$cm$^{-3}$)  shields the inner regions against starlight from massive stars. In the *molecular clouds* the high energy photons are completely extinguished by the concentration of dust grains. Under these conditions molecules are the main components in gas phase or in the form of ice.


## The role of interestellar dust

The molecular clouds are "giant laboratories", in which the interactions between gas and solid particles  at ultra low pressure and temperatures for long timescales enables solid state and surface chemistry  which are inaccessible in terrestrial experiments. The dusts provide the surface on which heterogeneous chemistry occurs and it is a substrate on which the icy mantles grow.

ISM molecular species can be formed through a network of gas phase reactions. The basic radiative association of two atoms under interstellar conditions is strongly forbidden by quantum mechanical selection rules, however three body association is possible. The problem is that the interstellar particle density is many order of magnitudes lower than the critical value needed for making those processes efficient, with the purely gas-phase networks failing to provide a mechanism that gives reasonable abundances of molecular species detected in interstellar clouds in star forming regions. 

For those molecules surface chemistry plays a crucial role. In case of a diatomic reaction, a first atom collides with an interstellar grain and is retained on the surface until a second atom arrives and reacts with it. This scenario constrains the possible reaction conditions: 
1. the first atom will have to stick to the grain surface and be retained sufficiently long to encounter the second atom and before desorption processes takes place. In the case where both atom are physisorbed and thermalized with the surface, the reaction takes place *via* the **Langmuir-Hinshelwood** mechanism{{< cite "langmuir1922">}},
2. an  excited atom can arrive at the surface near to an  adsorbed atom and a bond can be formed *via* the **Eley-Rideal** mechanism{{< cite "cuppenhornek2008">}}. 
3. The **hot atom** mechanism {{< cite "hotatom">}} is an intermediate process: thermal accommodation can occur through repeated interactions with the surface in which the kinetic energy of the atom is successively reduced. 

The primary role of the surface is to transfer energy from the hindered vibration-rotation of nascent molecule into the other modes. The energy can flow to the translational modes, leading to the desorption of a rovibrationally excited molecule. Alternatively, the nascent molecule may be weakly trapped in an excited state from which it will be later released or react with other species on the grain.  

The presence of dust is important both in star and planet formation. Star formation begins in cold, dark, dense molecular clouds, shielded from the interstellar radiation field. If the cloud has a sufficient mass, it may become unstable due to gravitational collapse, the grains help quenching the heat generated during the process and, once the critical mass is reached and the temperature in the star-forming region begins to rise, they participate to the planet formation stage. This involves collision between grains, driven by Van der Waals forces and kinetic collisions, that may lead to the compaction of loosely formed structures. 

## The interstellar ice 

Most of the grain surfaces are covered by *ice*, mostly $\ce{H2O}$ (with a density of 1000 molecule$\times$ cm$^{-3}$) {{< cite "watreview2013">}} with traces of other small species ($\ce{CH4}$,  $\ce{NH3}$, $\ce{CH3OH}$, $\ce{CO}$, $\ce{CO2}$, $\ce{N2}$, *etc*){{< cite "Gibb_2004">}}. Water ice is detected by its absorption in the pure O-H stretching mode in a broad feature peaking near 3 $\mu$m and by the bending and the  combination of libration modes from 4.5-6.3 $\mu$m{{< cite "gillett1973">}}. In the ISM the ice is  detected in the foreground material observed against a cool background star that has a prominent infrared continuum spectrum. Most of the detected ice seems to be in a high density amorphous form that has a sharp laboratory spectral feature at 3.1 $\mu$m. Porous ice  has a feature at 2.7 $\mu$m originating in a dangling $\ce{OH}$ bond, that is is not detected, suggesting its rarity in the ISM{{< cite "accolla2011">}}. The presence of impurities modifies the central position and line profiles of pure water ice features.  

Ice is inferred to be formed by the sticking of $\ce{O}$ atoms to the grain surface, and their hydrogenation  and retention as water molecules. Chemical pathways to form water ice may also include hydrogenation of $\ce{O2}$ and $\ce{O3}$, formed as well on the surface. In gas phase chemistry, water formation is hindered by the presence of carbon that takes up all the oxygen it can. 

The interaction between a solitary water molecule and the surface of a bare grain is different to that of a water molecule that is part of a monolayer: in the first case the molecule simply binds with the surface while  in the latter the binding is enhanced by H-bonding to adjacent water molecules. This finding is confirmed by experiments which showed that by increasing  the $\ce{H2O}$ coverage molecules desorb at higher temperatures{{< cite "cazaux2010">}}. In the cloud's dense regions a water monolayer tends to build up due to the flux of $\ce{O}$ atoms incident on the grain surfaces and hydrogen bonding among the molecule constituents dominates most desorption process and ensures ice mantel growth on the initial monolayer. 

Amorphous ice, known as amorphous solid water (ASW), is formed if the atoms on the surface  are frozen and their diffusion on the surface  is smaller than the lattice constant of the crystalline form of ice. The diffusion coefficient (*D*) of amorphous solid water is larger than hexagonal ice I$_h$ by one or two order of magnitude at the temperatures where ASW is preserved. The crystallization time scale estimated at 10 K is much larger than the cloud lifetime of 10$^7$ years. In our solar system the conditions for preserving ASW are found  outside  the Saturnian region{{< cite "kouchi1994">}}.

Due to the different conditions in different clouds, the polymorphs of water are relevant for ISM thermodynamical conditions. While the amorphous ice dominates below 130 K, metastable cubic phase (I$_c$) is formed at $130 < T< 170$ K, while I$_h$ forms a stable phase at $T > 170$ K {{< cite "hobbs1974">}}. <!--Figure \ref{fig:ices} displays the three different surfaces.--> Crystallization is enhanced by the presence of UV photons, producing a cycle of formation-dissociation-formation through exothermic reactions{{< cite "cazaux2010">}}. This excess of energy can be redistributed to the grain, starting a local nucleation processes.
